Misspecified Models}


\shorttitle{Moderated Mediation With Latent Variables}

\leftheader{Vieira and Rosseel}



\abstract{Moderated mediation is often not estimated with latent
variables, and approaches that can estimate it are rarely compared under
misspecification. In a preregistered simulation we evaluated four
approaches under a latent moderated mediation model, local
structural-after-measurement (LSAM), two system-wide methods, and factor
score regression as a baseline, across sample size, interaction
magnitude, reliability, predictor distribution, and nine
(mis)specification conditions. Under correct specification with normal
predictors, the first three recovered the interaction coefficient and
index of moderated mediation, differing modestly in bias, root mean
square error, coverage, standard errors, Type I error, and power,
whereas factor score regression underestimated both. Under
misspecification, LSAM was most robust, leaving some estimates
unaffected by omissions that biased system-wide approaches, although
most misspecifications degraded performance. }

\keywords{moderated mediation, structural equation modeling, latent
interaction, measurement error, model misspecification}

Mediation analysis is a well-known procedure in the behavioral and
social sciences (Charlton et al., 2021; Hayes, 2018; Pieters, 2017;
Preacher et al., 2007). In its basic and standard form, the effect of an
independent variable on an outcome is decomposed into a direct effect
and an indirect effect transmitted through a mediator, with all
variables observed. This single mediator model has been extended in a
number of directions, most relevant here being the case in which the
indirect effect is allowed to depend on a moderator (Edwards \& Lambert,
2007; Hayes, 2015; MacKinnon et al., 2007; Montoya, 2024; Preacher et
al., 2007).

In this context, a fundamental concern may arise from measurement. Many
constructs under investigation are not directly observed but are
measured indirectly through multiple items, that is, they are latent
variables (Bollen \& Hoyle, 2012). When such constructs are reduced to
summary scores (e.g., a sum or mean of the items) and analyzed as though
they were observed, a restrictive measurement structure is imposed
rather than tested (McNeish \& Wolf, 2020) and measurement error is left
unmodeled. This error can attenuate or inflate the indirect effect and
distort inferences about it (Cole \& Preacher, 2014; Hoyle \& Kenny,
1999), and it is especially consequential for the mediator, which acts
at once as a predictor and an outcome (Gonzalez \& MacKinnon, 2021). The
problem is compounded under moderation, where the product term is even
less reliable than either of its constituents (Bohrnstedt \& Marwell,
1978; Busemeyer \& Jones, 1983). To address this, structural equation
modeling (SEM) with latent variables has been advocated, especially in
the context of mediation (Cheung \& Lau, 2008; Falk \& Biesanz, 2015;
Ledgerwood \& Shrout, 2011; Montoya, 2024). With a correctly specified
measurement model, SEM estimates structural paths among latent factors
while accounting for indicator-specific residual variance (Bollen, 1989;
DeShon, 1998; Rhemtulla et al., 2020). Yet we also note that latent
variables do not necessarily improve power in general, whether for
interactions or not (Cham et al., 2012; Ledgerwood \& Shrout, 2011; Wang
\& Rhemtulla, 2021).

Moderated mediation with latent variables is not straightforward in
terms of statistical estimation. Indeed, a latent interaction\footnote{We
  use interaction and moderation interchangeably, as the focus is on
  estimation, but we highlight that they imply different interpretations
  and causal assumptions (VanderWeele, 2009). An interaction also need
  not be a product term, although that is the most common functional
  form used by researchers (Rimpler et al., 2026).} involves the product
of two latent variables, which is unobserved and nonnormal even when its
constituents are normal (Kelava \& Brandt, 2022). Following this, a
number of methods have been proposed to estimate nonlinear effects among
latent variables (e.g., Boker et al., 2023; Bollen, 1995; Kenny \& Judd,
1984; Klein \& Moosbrugger, 2000; Klein \& Muthén, 2007; Marsh et al.,
2004; Mooijaart \& Bentler, 2010). Most adopt a joint (``system-wide'')
estimation strategy, in which the measurement and structural parameters
are estimated simultaneously. This is the case of the two best-known
methods, latent moderated structural equations (LMS) and the
unconstrained product indicator (UPI) approach. In contrast,
structural-after-measurement (SAM) approaches (Cox \& Kelcey, 2021;
Holst \& Budtz-Jørgensen, 2020; Ng \& Chan, 2020; Rosseel et al., 2025;
Wall \& Amemiya, 2000, 2003) estimate the measurement model first and
the structural model afterward, with the measurement parameters held
fixed. Many of these approaches have so far rarely been applied in
practice, likely held back by limited software, slow estimation, and
error-prone implementations. With recent open-source implementations
(Holst \& Budtz-Jørgensen, 2020; Rosseel et al., 2025; Slupphaug et al.,
2025) and non-commercial software (Keller, 2025), this is likely to
change.

Beyond estimation, a further consideration concerns interpretation.
Mediation is a hypothesis about causal effects, and causal
interpretation requires identification assumptions that \emph{may} not
be verified from the data alone (e.g., Imai et al., 2010; Rohrer et al.,
2022). With latent variable models, further caution is needed (Morelli
et al., 2026; Sterner et al., 2024; VanderWeele \& Vansteelandt, 2022).
We focus on one that is central to interpretation: the estimated latent
variables must validly represent the intended constructs (Gonzalez \&
MacKinnon, 2021). In fact, when they do not, the model is subject to
interpretational confounding (Burt, 1976), a discrepancy between the
empirical meaning assigned to a latent variable through its estimated
measurement parameters and the meaning intended by the researcher. This
reflects an underlying property of system-wide estimation, namely that
the consequences of a misspecification are not confined to the part of
the model in which it occurs (Bainter \& Bollen, 2014; Bollen et al.,
2007; Kaplan, 1988). Burt (1976) proposed estimating the measurement
model first in order to separate the estimated latent variables and
their empirical meaning from the structural model that follows. SAM
approaches follow this strategy (e.g., Rosseel \& Loh, 2024), although
it does not imply that the structure is correct (VanderWeele \&
Vansteelandt, 2022).

Despite the relevance of misspecification, prior simulation work has
examined latent interaction estimation mainly under distributional
misspecification (Brandt et al., 2014; e.g., Cham et al., 2012; Lonati
et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2026), and in only two cases under
misspecification at the measurement level (Brandt et al., 2020; Navarro
\& Schermelleh-Engel, 2025). Among the approaches studied here, these
considered only UPI and LMS, and in limited settings. Moreover, when it
comes to (moderated) mediation, despite its popularity in applied
research, only a few studies have investigated it with latent variables,
and those assumed a correctly specified (and saturated) model (Cheung \&
Lau, 2017; Falk \& Biesanz, 2015; Feng et al., 2020). In this article,
we therefore evaluate four estimation approaches for a latent variable
moderated mediation model: local SAM (LSAM; Rosseel et al., 2025), LMS
(Kelava et al., 2011; Klein \& Moosbrugger, 2000), UPI (Kelava \&
Brandt, 2009; Marsh et al., 2004), and, as a baseline, factor score
regression (FSR). The first three have been explained extensively in
previous research and we refer readers to it (e.g., Brandt et al., 2020;
Kelava et al., 2011; Lin et al., 2010; Lonati et al., 2025; Rosseel et
al., 2025; Slupphaug et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2026).

\subsubsection{Present Research and
Contributions}\label{present-research-and-contributions}

This study makes two contributions. First, we evaluate LSAM alongside
LMS and UPI under both measurement and structural misspecification, in a
considerably broader design than previous studies, detailed in the next
section. The robustness of LSAM to model misspecification has itself
been examined only in linear models (Rosseel \& Loh, 2024), with later
research disputing it for measurement misspecifications and expecting it
to hold mainly for structural misspecifications in non-saturated
structural models (Robitzsch, 2022), a case not yet tested for models
with nonlinear effects. Second, we obtain inference for the index of
moderated mediation (imm; Hayes, 2015), a nonlinear function of the
structural parameters, with the Monte Carlo method (MacKinnon et al.,
2004; Preacher \& Selig, 2012). The latent variable studies above used
the bootstrap (Cheung \& Lau, 2017; Feng et al., 2020). Bootstrapping is
computationally expensive, and its variants differ modestly in
performance and rank differently depending on the criterion (Falk \&
Biesanz, 2015; Tibbe \& Montoya, 2022), whereas the Monte Carlo method
needs only the estimates and their covariance matrix. Whether it meets
the usual criteria for coverage and standard error accuracy for the imm
in the context under investigation has not been examined, and is the
question we address here. We do not compare it with the bootstrap.

\section{Simulation Design and Performance
Evaluation}\label{simulation-design-and-performance-evaluation}

In this section we describe the design of the simulation study and the
measures used to evaluate performance, following the preregistration and
some recent recommendations for the conduct and reporting of simulation
studies (Pawel et al., 2026; Siepe et al., 2024).

\subsubsection{Population Models and
Estimands}\label{population-models-and-estimands}

We study a moderated mediation model among four latent variables: an
exogenous predictor \(X\), a moderator \(W\), a mediator \(M\), and an
outcome \(Y\). The structural model that is fitted in every condition,
and that is the population model in the correctly specified condition,
is

\[
M = a_1 X + a_2 W + a_3\,(X \times W) + \zeta_M, \qquad
Y = b\,M + c'\,X + \zeta_Y.
\]

\noindent In the misspecified conditions the population departs from it
by one additional element, which the fitted model omits.
Figure~\ref{fig-model} shows the population model, where the measurement
and structural misspecifications studied below are drawn in gray.

The primary estimands are the interaction coefficient (\(a_3\)) and the
imm (i.e., \(a_3 b\)). The remaining structural coefficients (\(a_1\),
\(a_2\), \(b\), \(c'\)) and the twelve freely estimated factor loadings
serve as secondary estimands. Note the imm is a derived quantity rather
than a free parameter, hence its true value is computed as \(a_3 b\)
implied by the correctly specified model.

Population values were fixed at \(a_1 = 0.4\), \(a_2 = 0.3\),
\(b = 0.5\), and \(c' = 0.15\), with \(\operatorname{Cor}(X, W) = 0.3\).
The interaction coefficient \(a_3\) is a design factor (see below).
These coefficients, together with the structural disturbance variances,
were chosen so that under the baseline condition (normal exogenous
predictors and a correctly specified model) each dependent latent
variable has \(R^2 \approx 0.30\) at the interaction level
\(a_3 = 0.20\). Also note that, because \(a_3\) contributes to the
variance of \(M\), the explained variance is naturally higher with a
larger \(a_3\) and lower when its effect is null. The realized values in
every condition are reported in the supplementary materials. Each latent
variable is measured by four congeneric indicators with loadings
\(1.0\), \(0.8\), \(0.7\), and \(0.6\).

\subsubsection{Simulation Conditions}\label{simulation-conditions}

We employed a fully factorial design crossing five factors: sample size
(\(N \in \{100, 200, 300, 500\}\)), interaction magnitude
(\(a_3 \in \{0, 0.20, 0.40\}\)), measurement reliability (low \(= 0.5\),
high \(= 0.7\)), the distribution of the exogenous latent predictors
(normal, uniform, \(t_5\), and two \(\chi^2\) conditions with same- or
opposite-direction skewness), and the (mis)specification conditions
(nine cases; see Data Generation below). This yielded
\(4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 5 \times 9 = 1{,}080\) conditions, each
replicated 1,000 times.

Sample sizes cover the small to moderate range, where interaction terms
are estimated with limited power (Brandt et al., 2020; Lonati et al.,
2025). The interaction magnitudes include a null condition to assess
Type I error for the interaction coefficient and imm, and two nonzero
levels representing small and moderate latent moderation effects,
following previous research (Feng et al., 2020; Klein \& Moosbrugger,
2000). Reliability levels of \(0.5\) and \(0.7\) were chosen because
measurement error is amplified in product terms and is therefore
especially consequential for latent interaction estimation (Brandt et
al., 2020; Lonati et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2026). Nonnormality of
the latent predictors is both empirically common and methodologically
central for latent interaction approaches, whose distributional
assumptions differ across approaches (Cham et al., 2012; Klein \&
Moosbrugger, 2000; Klein \& Muthén, 2007; Lonati et al., 2025; Vieira et
al., 2026). The misspecification conditions follow the moderated
mediation framework for the two structural omissions (Preacher et al.,
2007) and prior latent interaction simulation work for the two
measurement omissions (Brandt et al., 2020). They were also chosen as
interpretable violations, acting respectively as a measured
mediator--outcome confounder (Imai et al., 2010), a conflated moderated
direct effect (Edwards \& Lambert, 2007), exposure-correlated
measurement error in the mediator (Fritz et al., 2016; VanderWeele et
al., 2012), and a shared unmeasured cause of a mediator and an outcome
indicator (Cole et al., 2007). The misspecification cases are detailed
below.

\subsubsection{Estimation Approaches}\label{estimation-approaches}

We used LSAM (Rosseel et al., 2025; Rosseel \& Loh, 2024; Vieira et al.,
2026), with the measurement model estimated by maximum likelihood (ML)
in the first step and with standard errors obtained as in Vieira et al.
(2026) (see their Appendix A). In this case, the interacting predictors
\(X\) and \(W\) shared a single measurement block, while \(M\) and \(Y\)
formed their own blocks. We further used LMS (Klein \& Moosbrugger,
2000) and the extended UPI approach with double mean centering (Kelava
\& Brandt, 2009; Lin et al., 2010; Marsh et al., 2004), the latter
estimated by ML. For both, robust standard errors were obtained with the
Huber--White sandwich estimator. As a baseline, we also used
FSR\footnote{We note that the approach of Skrondal and Laake (2001),
  used for latent interactions by Ng and Chan (2020), would be a more
  principled choice, in which regression (Thurstone) factor scores are
  used for the explanatory variables and Bartlett factor scores for the
  response variables. However, as noted previously (Devlieger et al.,
  2016), this does not work in models where a variable is both
  explanatory and a response, as the mediator is here.}. Specifically,
Bartlett factor scores were computed from a confirmatory factor model of
the four latent variables estimated by ML, and the structural model was
estimated on the scores by ML, which gives the ordinary least squares
estimates, with standard errors solely from this second step.

\subsubsection{Data Generation}\label{data-generation}

The supplementary materials describe the complete data generating
mechanism and its calibration in detail. Briefly, the latent variables
\(X\) and \(W\) were drawn from a bivariate distribution with
\(\operatorname{Cor}(X, W) = \rho = 0.3\), constructed with the
vine-to-anything (VITA) algorithm (Grønneberg et al., 2022). Five
exogenous distributions were examined, each standardized to mean \(0\)
and variance \(1\). The normal condition draws
\(X, W \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) through a Gaussian copula, whereas the
remaining four combine nonnormal margins with the Clayton copula:
uniform, \(t_5\), and two \(\chi^2(2)\) conditions in which \(X\) and
\(W\) are skewed in the same or in opposite directions. The nonnormal
conditions therefore differ from the normal condition in the dependence
between the predictors as well as in their margins. As a sensitivity
analysis, they were repeated with a Gaussian copula to separate the two
(see the supplementary materials). For each distribution, the bivariate
population was constructed once and every replication was sampled from
it. Then, \(M\) and \(Y\) were generated from the structural equations
above with independent Gaussian disturbances
\(\zeta_M \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \psi_M)\) and
\(\zeta_Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \psi_Y)\), and the four indicators
(\(j = 1, \dots, 4\)) of each latent variable
\(\eta \in \{X, W, M, Y\}\) were formed as
\(z_{\eta j} = \lambda_j \eta + \delta_{\eta j}\), with loadings
\(\lambda = (1, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6)\) and Gaussian errors
\(\delta_{\eta j} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \theta_{\eta j})\) whose variances
\(\theta_{\eta j}\) were set to give the target reliability
\(\omega \in \{0.5, 0.7\}\) under normal predictors and held fixed
across distributions.

The nine (mis)specification conditions comprise one correctly specified
condition and four types of misspecification, each at two calibrated
magnitudes (standardized \(0.25\) and \(0.50\)), similar to Brandt et
al. (2020). The two structural ones are an omitted direct effect of the
moderator on the outcome (\(c_2\)) and an omitted moderated direct
effect (\(c_3\)). The two in the measurement model are a cross-loading
of a mediator indicator on \(X\) (\texttt{cl}) and a residual covariance
between a mediator and an outcome indicator (\texttt{rc}). In every case
the effect is present in the population but omitted from the estimated
model.

\subsubsection{Convergence, Outliers, and Inadmissible
Solutions}\label{convergence-outliers-and-inadmissible-solutions}

Following previous research (Lonati et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2026),
replications were screened in two sequential steps, and a replication
that failed either step for any estimation approach was removed for all
of them, so that every approach is compared on the same replications.
First, a solution was treated as nonconverged when the optimizer
reported nonconvergence or when any estimate, standard error, or
confidence interval bound was missing or non-finite. Second, among the
remaining replications, outliers were identified per condition and
approach as estimates of the imm or a structural coefficient more than
three interquartile ranges beyond the quartiles, or with a standard
error above \(10\)\footnote{The standard error criterion additionally
  removed 113 replications (about \(0.01\%\)), almost all for UPI at the
  smallest sample size with low reliability.}. We also recorded, without
removing them, inadmissible solutions, in which a residual variance was
nonpositive or the covariance matrix of the latent variables and
disturbances was not positive definite. The same was done for converged
solutions whose information matrix was not positive definite. Their
frequencies and the sensitivity of the results to the outlier screening
are reported in the supplementary materials.

\subsubsection{Performance Measures}\label{performance-measures}

Performance was assessed with the measures implemented in the
\emph{simhelpers} package (Joshi \& Pustejovsky, 2025), computed for the
imm, the structural coefficients, and the loadings. These measures, with
their empirical and Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE) calculations, are
listed in Table~\ref{tbl-performance}. Note that the MCSEs are obtained
in closed form where available and by jackknife otherwise. In addition,
where applicable, we report the rejection rate (Type I error when
\(a_3 = 0\), power otherwise).

Inference for the imm followed the Monte Carlo method as mentioned
before: for \(d = 1, \dots, D\) (with \(D = 20{,}000\)) we drew

\[
\bigl(a_3^{(d)}, b^{(d)}\bigr) \sim
\mathcal{N}_2\!\bigl((\hat a_3, \hat b),\, \hat\Sigma_{a_3 b}\bigr),
\qquad
\mathrm{imm}^{(d)} = a_3^{(d)}\, b^{(d)},
\]

\noindent where \(\hat\Sigma_{a_3 b}\) is the estimated covariance of
\((\hat a_3, \hat b)\), and took the \(2.5\)th and \(97.5\)th empirical
percentiles of \(\{\mathrm{imm}^{(d)}\}_{d=1}^{D}\) as the interval. The
structural coefficients and loadings use Wald-based intervals from the
same estimated covariance matrix of the parameter estimates.

\section{Results}\label{results}

We present the results in four parts. After the convergence results, we
report the interaction coefficient and the imm, first under correct
structural and measurement specification and then under
misspecification. We then turn to the remaining structural coefficients
(i.e., \(a_1\), \(a_2\), \(b\), and \(c'\)), and we close with the
estimated factor loadings, focusing mainly on that of the cross-loaded
indicator (\(m_3\)). Due to the large number of conditions, the figures
in the main text show a subset. Complete results for every condition,
and detailed convergence and outlier information, are available in the
supplementary materials.

\subsection{Convergence, Outliers, and Inadmissible
Solutions}\label{convergence-outliers-and-inadmissible-solutions-1}

LSAM and FSR converged in every replication of every condition. UPI and
LMS converged in at least 98\% of replications in most conditions, with
lowest rates of 93\% for UPI (\(N = 100\), low reliability, \(\chi^2\)
predictors) and 89\% for LMS (\(N = 500\), high reliability, \(t_5\)
predictors). Outliers were below 1\% in most conditions and reached at
most 3\% for LSAM, LMS, and FSR and 11\% for UPI (\(N = 100\), low
reliability, \(\chi^2\) predictors). Inadmissible solutions arose almost
exclusively for UPI, mainly at \(N = 100\) with low reliability. They
were retained, and excluding them changes the results only for UPI in
these conditions. Details are in Tables 3 and 4 of the supplementary
materials.

\subsection{Correct Structural and Measurement
Specification}\label{correct-structural-and-measurement-specification}

We show the imm for every measure, and the interaction coefficient
alongside it where the two differ, that is, for the standard errors and
coverage. Relative bias is shown at both nonzero interaction magnitudes
(\(a_3 = 0.2\) and \(0.4\)), the standard errors, coverage, and power at
the smaller one, and the Type I error under no interaction
(\(a_3 = 0\)).

\subsubsection{Relative Bias and Relative
RMSE}\label{relative-bias-and-relative-rmse}

An absolute relative bias of at most 0.10 (10\%) was considered
acceptable, following previous research (Brandt et al., 2014; Vieira et
al., 2026). LSAM and UPI recover the imm with acceptable bias in nearly
all conditions (Figure~\ref{fig-baseline-bias-imm}). Their bias exceeds
the acceptable range only at \(N = 100\) with low reliability and
\(\chi^2\) predictors, where it reaches \(-0.11\) for LSAM and \(-0.18\)
for UPI. With normal, uniform, and \(t_5\) predictors, the bias of LMS
is acceptable as well, with one exception at \(N = 100\) (\(-0.16\)).
With \(\chi^2\) predictors, however, its bias persists across sample
sizes, reaching 0.15 under same skewness (\(a_3 = 0.2\), low
reliability) and about \(-0.10\) under opposite skewness
(\(a_3 = 0.4\)). FSR underestimates the imm in every condition, by 21\%
to 35\% at high reliability and by 41\% to 56\% at low reliability,
irrespective of sample size. The interaction coefficient shows the same
pattern (see Figure 3 of the supplementary materials). Among LSAM, LMS,
and UPI, the relative RMSE is lowest for LMS and highest for UPI in
nearly every condition. That of LSAM is close to that of LMS (on average
5\% to 8\% higher), except with opposite-skewness \(\chi^2\) predictors,
where it is about 1.4 times as large (see Figures 12 to 15 of the
supplementary materials).

slightly lower and falls below the range more often (0.89 at the
lowest). For FSR, coverage decreases with sample size, because the

at \(N = 500\) with low reliability. At the larger interaction magnitude

\subsubsection{Hypothesis Testing (Type I Error and
Power)}\label{hypothesis-testing-type-i-error-and-power}

Type I error rates between 2.5\% and 7.5\% were considered acceptable.
At \(a_3 = 0\), LMS is liberal in 17 of the 40 conditions, nearly all
with \(t_5\) and \(\chi^2\) predictors, with rates up to 0.10
(Figure~\ref{fig-test}). LSAM and UPI are slightly liberal in a few
conditions at high reliability (up to 0.09 and 0.08). At low
reliability, UPI is instead conservative in 10 conditions (0.01 at the
lowest), largely those in which its standard errors are overestimated.
FSR keeps the Type I error acceptable in all but one condition (0.09).

At \(a_3 = 0.2\), power increases with sample size and reliability, and
reaches 0.80 for most distributions only from \(N = 300\) at high
reliability and at \(N = 500\) at low reliability. LSAM and LMS have
similar power with normal, uniform, and \(t_5\) predictors, whereas with
\(\chi^2\) predictors LMS has higher power than LSAM and UPI. The
difference is largest with opposite-skewness \(\chi^2\) predictors at
low reliability, where at \(N = 500\) power is 0.80 for LMS, 0.46 for
LSAM, and 0.31 for UPI. The higher power of LMS in this condition comes
with a slightly liberal Type I error (0.08), but its estimates are also
more precise. Indeed, with opposite-skewness \(\chi^2\) predictors, the
standard deviation of the LSAM and UPI estimates is 1.3 to 1.9 times
that of LMS. FSR has power close to that of LMS. The interaction
coefficient gives nearly identical results (see Figures 32 to 35 of the
supplementary materials).

\subsection{Structural and Measurement
Misspecification}\label{structural-and-measurement-misspecification}

Recall that the imm is the product of the interaction coefficient
(\(a_3\)), from the mediator equation, and \(b\), from the outcome
equation. The structural omissions (\(c_2\) and \(c_3\)) are in the
outcome equation, to which \(b\) belongs. The measurement omissions (the
cross-loading and the residual covariance) involve a mediator indicator,
and the mediator enters both equations. We show the relative bias of the
interaction coefficient and the imm at \(N = 500\), because the bias
induced by an omission changes little with sample size, and the standard
errors and Type I error of the imm at the smallest and largest sample
size.

\subsubsection{Relative Bias and Relative
RMSE}\label{relative-bias-and-relative-rmse-1}

Both structural omissions bias the imm upward through \(b\) for LSAM,
LMS, and UPI, increasingly with the omission magnitude, but they differ
in whether the bias also reaches the interaction coefficient
(Figure~\ref{fig-mis-bias}). Under \(c_2\) the interaction coefficient
is largely unaffected, as expected, except for LMS at low reliability
(up to 0.15 in absolute value), and the bias of the imm is similar for
the three approaches (on average 0.40 to 0.49 at the medium magnitude).
Under \(c_3\), LMS and UPI also overestimate the interaction coefficient
(by 0.30 on average at the medium magnitude and low reliability),
whereas LSAM does not. The bias of the imm is accordingly smaller for
LSAM (0.28 on average at both reliability levels) than for LMS and UPI
(about 0.50 at high and 0.80 at low reliability). The medium
cross-loading biases the two components of the imm in opposite
directions for LMS and UPI, attenuating the interaction coefficient (on
average by 0.21 and 0.26 at low reliability) and inflating \(b\) (by
0.40), so that the bias of the imm remains largely acceptable, except
for LMS with \(\chi^2\) predictors (up to 0.35). For LSAM both biases
are smaller (0.07 and 0.14). The residual covariance leaves the
interaction coefficient unaffected and biases \(b\), and thereby the
imm, slightly upward for the three approaches (by about 0.09 at the
medium magnitude and low reliability). For FSR, the structural omissions
move the attenuated imm toward zero (on average 0.03 under the medium
\(c_2\) at high reliability), which reflects two biases that offset each
other and not accurate estimation. The relative RMSE follows the bias
and is accordingly lowest for LSAM under the medium \(c_3\) (at
\(N = 500\) and low reliability 0.62, against 1.04 for LMS and 1.11 for
UPI; see Figures 12 to 15 of the supplementary materials). For the
remaining sample sizes and the larger interaction magnitude
(\(a_3 = 0.4\)), see Figures 4 to 11 of the supplementary materials.

\subsubsection{Coverage}\label{coverage-1}

Under misspecification the interval is centered on a biased estimate,
and coverage therefore mirrors the bias reported above. It decreases
wherever an omission induces bias, more so with increasing sample size,
and is as under correct specification where an omission induces little
bias. At \(N = 500\) under the medium structural omissions, the coverage

Figures 24 to 31 of the supplementary materials).

\subsubsection{Hypothesis Testing (Type I Error and
Power)}\label{hypothesis-testing-type-i-error-and-power-1}

For LSAM, the Type I error is not affected by the omissions and remains
as under correct specification, slightly liberal in some conditions at
high reliability (up to 0.10; Figure~\ref{fig-mis-typeI}). LMS is
liberal in 152 of the 360 conditions, nearly all with \(t_5\) and

and 0.33 at \(N = 500\). UPI remains conservative at low reliability, as

where its rate increases with sample size (up to 0.25). Power changes
little for LSAM and FSR. For LMS and UPI it increases under \(c_3\),
following the bias in the interaction coefficient, and for UPI it
decreases under the medium cross-loading. Power should not be
interpreted where the estimate is biased (see Figures 32 to 35 of the

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

We evaluated four estimation approaches in a latent variable moderated
mediation model, the two-step LSAM, the system-wide LMS and UPI, and FSR
as a baseline, under correct specification and under structural and
measurement misspecifications as well as distributional violations.

Under correct specification with normal predictors, LSAM, LMS, and UPI
recovered the imm and the interaction coefficient with acceptable bias
and with coverage close to nominal, whereas FSR attenuated the imm by
about a quarter at high and about half at low reliability. Across the
predictor distributions, LMS had the lowest relative RMSE and the
highest power, but with \(\chi^2\) predictors it was biased and with
\(t_5\) and \(\chi^2\) predictors its Type I error was inflated, as
expected given its assumption of normal latent predictors (Lonati et
al., 2025).\footnote{The results of LMS with nonnormal predictors also
  depended on the copula. When the predictors were combined through a
  Gaussian instead of the Clayton copula, its bias was larger with
  \(\chi^2\) predictors (e.g., 0.32 against 0.15 with same-skewness
  predictors at \(a_3 = 0.2\), \(N = 500\), and low reliability) and
  smaller with uniform and \(t_5\) predictors, in line with how far the
  mixed moments of the predictors beyond the second order are from
  normality under each copula (see Figure 1 and Table 7 of the
  supplementary materials).} Among the three, LSAM yielded Type I error
closest to nominal, at some cost in relative RMSE and power, while UPI
had the highest relative RMSE and the lowest power, with acceptable bias
except at the smallest sample size with low reliability, where it was
also the least stable. Its standard errors were overestimated in small
samples at low reliability, largely because of a few extreme values, and
UPI and LSAM struggled most under opposite-skewness \(\chi^2\)
predictors.

Under misspecification, LSAM was less affected than the system-wide
approaches, without being robust to every omission. The structural
omissions in the outcome equation (\(c_2\), \(c_3\)) biased the
coefficients of that equation, and thereby the imm, for all approaches
but left the mediator equation, and hence the interaction coefficient,
unaffected under LSAM, whereas the system-wide approaches also carried
them into the mediator equation, biasing \(a_2\) under \(c_2\) and the
interaction coefficient under \(c_3\). The cross-loading (\texttt{cl})
was the most damaging for the structural coefficients. Under the
system-wide approaches it biased most of them, including the interaction
coefficient, while under LSAM it affected mainly \(a_1\) and \(c'\). The
imm, however, was little affected. The residual covariance (\texttt{rc})
had little effect. The Type I error of LSAM was unchanged by the
omissions, whereas that of LMS rose to 0.33. Where bias was substantial,
it was smaller under LSAM, except for \(c'\) under the structural
omissions, and it increased with the magnitude of the omission.

Regarding the imm, the Monte Carlo method delivered standard errors and
confidence intervals that behaved like those of the interaction
coefficient, with the deviations at low reliability and small samples
reflecting the standard errors of the underlying estimates. Under
correct specification, the method was thus adequate by the criteria
applied to standard errors and coverage. Under misspecification, its
coverage dropped with the bias of its constituents despite wider
intervals.

Moreover, these patterns are broadly consistent with previous research.
The behavior of LMS and UPI under nonnormality and their stability under
normality reproduce earlier findings (Brandt et al., 2014; Cham et al.,
2012; Lonati et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2026), as does that of LSAM
(Rosseel et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2026). The measurement
misspecification results align with Brandt et al. (2020), where an
omitted cross-loading was likewise damaging for every estimator,
including UPI. Navarro and Schermelleh-Engel (2025) likewise found the
latent interaction effect underestimated under omitted positive
cross-loadings with LMS. Even so, Brandt et al. (2020) found no approach
free of bias and none uniformly best, as we also observed. Lastly, the
contrast between the structural and the measurement omissions matches
the expectation of Robitzsch (2022) that the advantage of SAM lies in
structural rather than measurement misspecification, since the
cross-loading biased LSAM as well, whereas the structural omissions left
its mediator equation untouched.

For applied researchers, these results suggest the following within the
conditions studied. When the latent predictors can be assumed normal,
LMS is the most efficient choice. When they may be nonnormal, LSAM is
preferable, because it kept the Type I error closest to nominal, at the
price of lower power with strongly skewed predictors. LSAM was also less
affected by misspecification without being robust to it, since the imm
was still biased under both structural omissions and the omitted
cross-loading biased its estimates as well. UPI had acceptable bias but
the lowest power, and it is not advisable in small samples with low
reliability. FSR is not recommended, since it attenuated the imm by a
quarter to a half, as is known to happen when the measurement error in
the scores is ignored (Devlieger et al., 2016; Ng \& Chan, 2020; Rosseel
et al., 2025). With an interaction of the size studied here, a power of
0.80 required about 300 observations at high and 500 at low reliability
for most predictor distributions. Still, misspecification had strong
consequences across all approaches, and many of the omissions studied
may be highly plausible in applied contexts. No approach protected
against an omitted path in the outcome equation or a misspecified
measurement model, and both should therefore be examined before the imm
is interpreted, which the separate measurement step of LSAM makes
natural. Finally, although we evaluated the estimation approaches from a
statistical perspective, the moderated mediation model itself rests on
strong causal assumptions (Imai et al., 2010; Rohrer et al., 2022). Good
statistical performance in certain conditions is therefore not, on its
own, a justification for a causal claim, and we direct interested
readers to the developing literature on the causal interpretation of
mediation (Imai et al., 2010; Morelli et al., 2026).
